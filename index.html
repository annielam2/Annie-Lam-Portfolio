HCDE 313 — User-Centered Design Research Fall 2025 Solo project Observe → Ask → Validate

Rain isn't just weather.

Helping UW students protect themselves from rain — a user research project combining field observation, semi-structured interviews, and a validating survey to find out what would actually help.

Focus

Rain protection and mobility on UW's Seattle campus.

Role

Research planning, field study, interviews, survey design and synthesis.

Tools

Google Forms, Otter.ai, Google Sheets, FigJam.

Deliverables

Field study report, interview top-line report, survey plan and findings presentation.

01 / Problem

Owning gear isn't the same as being protected.

Seattle sees rain nearly half the year, and the rainiest months — fall through spring — overlap almost entirely with the academic calendar. Students are constantly moving between buildings, transit, and home in wet conditions.

An Axios Seattle survey found that 82% of people own an umbrella, but only 6% use one exclusively when it rains. Ownership isn't the issue — consistent, convenient use is. That gap became the starting point.

Design question: How can we help people on campus protect themselves from rain?

02 / Approach

Three studies, each answering the last.

Each phase narrowed the question and tested what the previous one couldn't answer on its own — observation to see behavior, interviews to understand motivation, and a survey to check whether it generalized.

Phase 1 — Observe
Field study

Three 30-minute sessions in Red Square during light-to-moderate rain.

Phase 2 — Ask
Interviews

Three semi-structured interviews, about 20 minutes each.

Phase 3 — Validate
Survey

A 12-question survey, 17 respondents, via Google Forms.

03 / Field study

Reading rain behavior in Red Square.

Red Square is one of UW's busiest and least sheltered spaces — brick paving, minimal overhead coverage, and a direct route between Odegaard, Suzzallo, and Kane. I observed from under the Odegaard awning on October 12, 18, and 19, taking structured notes across six focus areas: built environment, possessions, tools and technology, traffic, information access, and overall experience.

01

Improvised rain protection was the norm

Most people used whatever they had on hand — folders, tote bags, sleeves, hoods worn half-up. Even students carrying rain jackets often left the hood down.

"Female student leaving Suzzallo wearing oversized hoodie and leggings, hood halfway on, holding tote bag over her head while fast-walking toward Odegaard."Field notes, session 1
02

Minimal coverage created predictable congestion

With almost no covered path between buildings, people funneled toward the few awnings — pausing to reorganize belongings or wait for friends. The Odegaard entrance became a reliable bottleneck.

"Students often crowd at the Ode entrance while waiting for someone to scan in, then all collectively walk in."Field notes, session 2
03

Rain reshaped, but didn't erase, social behavior

Groups were visibly more relaxed than solo walkers — chatting, laughing, occasionally sharing an umbrella — and often paused under awnings together before splitting off.

04 / Interviews

Why students make these choices.

Observation showed me what people did, not why. Three 20-minute interviews with third-year students let me follow up on the exact behaviors I'd seen — the folder-as-umbrella, the hood left down, the shared umbrella.

Refined question: How can we make rain protection on campus more accessible and convenient, so students can move more comfortably in rainy conditions?

01

Unsafe, inefficient infrastructure

All three participants independently named Red Square, brick pathways, grates, and Drumheller Fountain's mist as the worst spots — and all three described slowing down or rerouting to avoid slipping.

"[Red Square is] not built for the weather conditions Seattle has."P1
02

Preparedness is about convenience, style, and norms — not lack of gear

Everyone had some form of protection but was underprepared at least some of the time. Umbrellas felt inconvenient, didn't match an outfit, or carried a stigma of "not being local."

"It depends on my outfit of the day… it's just vibes, with the vibes."P3, on choosing a rain jacket
03

Rain disrupts social experience — in different directions

One participant found company more stressful in the rain; the other two found it a relief.

"[Sharing an umbrella is] cozy and fun. It's a human interaction."P3

I coded the interview transcripts into four thematic maps, pulling design opportunities from participants' own words.

Click any map to enlarge.

06 / Recommendations

Where the research points.

The same three ideas surfaced independently at every phase — observed in the field, explained in interviews, and confirmed in the survey. That convergence is what makes them the priority.

Field · Interviews · Survey
01 — Fix Red Square's surfaces first

Improve traction and drainage on the brick paving, and add pathway lighting — the highest-confidence, safety-critical fix.

Field · Interviews · Survey
02 — Pilot communal umbrellas

Branded umbrella stations at building entrances address the convenience and stigma barriers — 65% said they'd likely use one.

Field · Interviews · Survey
03 — Connect covered walkways

Modular canopies linking Kane, Odegaard, and Suzzallo close the exposure gap — rated "extremely useful" by over half of respondents.

A fourth idea — semi-sheltered social seating, so the pauses I observed under awnings have a better place to happen — came out of the interviews but wasn't tested in the survey. It's the natural next thing to validate.

07 / Reflection

Checking my own assumptions.

Each phase caught something the previous one couldn't. The field study couldn't explain why a student would rather get soaked than carry an umbrella; the interviews couldn't tell me how common that reasoning was; and the survey couldn't have surfaced "umbrella stigma" as a concept if the interviews hadn't found it first.

As a Seattle native, I assumed people here just accept getting a little wet. The interviews complicated that — it's a mix of convenience, style, and a real social norm. Recognizing that bias early, and writing open-ended follow-ups instead of leading ones, kept me from just confirming what I already believed.

Next time I'd recruit a more varied pool — all three interviewees were the same age and year — and observe a wider range of rain intensity. The most unexpectedly fun part: nearly everyone had a specific, slightly embarrassing rain story ready to go.

HCDE 303 20 weeks · Jan – May 2025 Team 8 SEAAK (4 members) Mentor: Prof. Arpita B.

Dubcare.

A mobile and web solution that helps UW students seek urgent medical care for non-life-threatening illnesses or injuries — making the entire process of seeking care informative, efficient, and comfortable, while educating students about their insurance and the resources available to them.

Course

HCDE 303 — Foundations in Human Centered Design, core HCDE curriculum.

Team

Elle Yamamoto, Selena Liu, Annie Huang, Annie Lam.

Tools

Figma, FigJam — research synthesis through high-fidelity prototyping.

Outcome

High-fidelity prototype + onboarding module concept, reviewed by industry experts.

01 / Research

Three barriers, one system.

After researching UW students' experiences in navigating urgent care and insurance plans, three key barriers emerged that needed to be addressed:

01

Insurance match-making is broken

Students struggle to find the corresponding urgent care that actually takes their insurance — meaning the search itself becomes a barrier to care.

02

Non-transparent bills & fees

Costs and fees aren't disclosed until after the appointment, leaving students blindsided by bills they didn't know to expect.

03

No reliable wait-time system

Students can't manage their schedule around urgent care visits because there's no accurate wait-time information available.

02 / Requirements

What the design had to do.

Design Requirements
  • Inform the patient of costs before seeking care
  • Present medical information in one organized place
  • Filter urgent care locations by the patient's insurance
  • Guide patients on what to ask at the clinic
  • Allow patients to join a waitlist for appointments
Design Goals
  • Encourage preparedness before a medical need exists
  • Make healthcare information accessible throughout the care process
  • Educate students on where and how to seek help
  • Reduce cognitive load during high-stress decisions
  • Build healthcare literacy as a long-term outcome
03 / Personas

Two students, two journeys.

Our group created two personas, Ella and Max, based on insights gathered from urgent care experience interviews. They captured distinct patterns: students who'd recently transitioned away from parent-managed healthcare, and students navigating an unfamiliar healthcare system on their own.

We also built a user journey map following Max through his urgent care experience. The map traced not just the steps he took but the emotional arc alongside them — showing where design could intervene to lower stress, not just speed things up.

04 / Concepts

Three storyboards, one winner.

We sketched three distinct concepts to address the pain points, then evaluated which had the most reach:

Concept A
Husky ID QR Code

A scannable code linked to the student's insurance and health profile — surfaces relevant info at the point of care.

Concept B
Visual Urgent Care Map

A map-based interface filtering clinics by insurance acceptance, distance, hours, and wait time.

Concept C → Chosen
Comprehensive Website

One platform combining clinic discovery, insurance education, cost transparency, and waitlist signup.

After expert review with John Harris and Daan Lindhout, we made a key decision: combine all three concepts into one cohesive solution. The integrated product would include the visual urgent care map, the comprehensive website, the Husky ID code checklist — plus a new student onboarding process that prepares students before a medical emergency, walking them through insurance, common medical terms, and how to navigate urgent care.

[This project addresses a] very common problem that's much broader than the student population. Other user types could benefit greatly from this.

— John Harris & Daan Lindhout, External Reviewers
05 / Competitive analysis

Learning from existing systems.

To understand the existing landscape, we analyzed four products that overlap with our problem space: UW Student Health Website, Regence BlueShield, the UW Insurance Handbook, and the Medicaid Website.

Strong patterns to borrow: clear layouts, effective icon use, translation features, simple language, cost estimation tools, and phone support pathways. Patterns to avoid: redundant links, dense unbroken text, missing alt text, poor mobile optimization, vague navigation, and unexplained abbreviations.

The gap in the market was clear — none of the existing products combined urgent-care discovery with insurance literacy in a student-friendly format. That's the space DubCare moved into.

06 / Iteration

Low → mid → high fidelity.

Information architecture. We mapped content, features, and navigation flow before drawing any screens.

Low-fidelity prototype. Built in Figma after expert feedback, this rough draft helped us feel out how features should be organized.

Mid-fidelity prototype. We introduced the DubCare brand, color, and real content.

Mid-fidelity user testing surfaced clear directions:

What testers liked
  • Minimal, clean design
  • Familiarity with existing map apps
  • Good use of color and space
  • Clarity of the layout
What needed fixing
  • "Best match" sorting with filter context
  • Public transit option on the map
  • ER vs. urgent care section more visible
  • Waitlist confirmation messaging
  • Clearer wait-time and hours display
  • Module labels caused confusion
  • Inconsistent module sizing
07 / Expert review

The second round.

John Harris reviewed our mid-fidelity prototype and praised the "simple, clean design… good use of space and color choice." But he also surfaced real issues:

Information architecture — Critical ER vs. urgent care info was buried behind multiple taps; clinic-page hierarchy didn't match user-need priority.

Interface — Purple/gold color overlap created confusion when applied to different button states; clinic comparison on map cards was difficult.

Usability — The site's purpose wasn't immediately clear on first visit; learning modules didn't align well with the user journey.

Based on this feedback we refined the design system (typography, color themes), reduced information density on the landing and learning pages, visualized the Urgent Care vs. ER checklist, and organized components into a more systematic flow.

08 / High-fidelity prototype

Try it yourself.

The final round refined the information architecture, reworked the resource page content, and finalized prototype interactions. The prototype below is live — click through it the way a student would when deciding where to get care.

09 / Retrospective

What 20 weeks taught us.

Over the course of the project we transformed an initial research question about urgent care accessibility into a functional high-fidelity prototype with room for future development. The most significant design challenge was balancing two needs in one IA: helping users make informed decisions while also connecting them quickly to services.

If we were starting again, we'd conduct deeper research on the pain points within the urgent care process itself, and dig further into levels of student healthcare literacy to better inform the educational portion of the design.

The most rewarding aspect was seeing how our solution has potential to address a real need in the student community — and how each team member's strengths, from research to visual design, compounded over the 20 weeks to take DubCare from a question to a working prototype.

The original site lacked strong hierarchy and didn't fully reflect the credibility expected of a financial firm. I redesigned it to create a modern, trustworthy, and conversion-friendly user experience.

Role

UX/UI Design Intern — audit, research, IA, wireframing, visual design, full rebuild.

Timeline

March – June 2025 (four months).

Tools

Framer, Divi, Ghost, Figma, WordPress.

Outcome

01 / Overview

A financial brand in need of trust signals.

Mercer Island Capital is a financial insights brand focused on long-term investing and wealth education. The original website lacked strong hierarchy and didn't fully reflect the credibility expected of a financial firm.

I was brought in as the UX/UI Design Intern to redesign the site — creating a modern, trustworthy, and conversion-friendly experience that matched the firm's positioning.

02 / The problem

Five issues compounding against trust.

The original site had several UX issues that limited clarity and trust:

Visual & layout problems
  • Weak visual hierarchy — content was hard to scan
  • Inconsistent spacing reduced professionalism
  • Text-heavy sections lacked rhythm and structure
  • Design didn't match financial-services expectations
Conversion problems
  • No strong CTA to guide users toward conversion
  • Unclear next-step pathways for prospective clients
  • Backend (Divi) made even small changes difficult
  • Friction compounded user uncertainty

Together, these problems created friction and reduced user confidence in the brand — exactly the opposite of what a wealth-management firm needs.

03 / Goals

Three goals, one direction.

Goal 01
Improve clarity

Strengthen visual hierarchy, spacing, and overall readability so users can scan and understand quickly.

Goal 02
Build client trust

Create a more professional design aligned with top financial institutions — visual credibility as a baseline.

Goal 03
Increase conversion

Add clearer CTAs and structured guidance to support user action and move prospective clients forward.

04 / Audit + competitive analysis

Learning from the leaders.

I began by reviewing the structure of the original website, which was built in WordPress using the Divi theme. The backend was overly complex and made even small layout changes difficult — which revealed deeper issues with hierarchy, spacing, readability, and content flow.

To better understand user expectations for financial brands, I examined leading wealth-management websites — JP Morgan, Millennial Wealth, and Independent Financial Partners. Five trust-building patterns emerged consistently across all three:

What top firms do
  • Clear hero messaging
  • Strong spacing and hierarchy
  • Professional typography
  • Clean, scannable sections
  • Prominent CTAs ("Get Started," "Talk to an Advisor")
What this told me
  • Trust is signaled by restraint, not richness
  • Whitespace is part of the message
  • Typography choices alone can shift credibility
  • CTAs need to feel inevitable, not desperate
  • Information density should taper, not surge
05 / Prototyping & tool exploration

Try four tools, pick the right one.

Before committing to a rebuild, I explored multiple approaches to find the best platform and workflow:

01

Ghost.io experiment

Tried Ghost to evaluate a simpler publishing system — promising for content-first sites, but limited for the structured layout the firm needed.

02

Figma wireframes

Built initial wireframes in Figma to test layout structure and section flow — useful for stakeholder review before any code or theme decisions.

03

Hand-coded layouts

Tested custom HTML/CSS layouts to evaluate full customization — clarified what was possible outside a page-builder's constraints.

04

Tool comparison

Compared how different tools handled responsiveness, spacing, and flexibility — the three dimensions where the original Divi build had failed.

These experiments clarified the technical and design limitations of the original build — and gave me the evidence to argue for a full rebuild rather than incremental patches.

06 / Rebuild

From scratch, on purpose.

After evaluating all alternatives, I decided to rebuild the entire website from scratch using WordPress rather than modifying the existing Divi structure. Starting fresh allowed me to:

Identity
Strong visual identity

Establish a professional, financial-industry tone from the foundation rather than retrofitting it.

Hierarchy
Modern, clean hierarchy

Build the IA around what users want to know in the order they want to know it.

Whitespace
Improved spacing & readability

Give every section breathing room — density was costing the brand credibility.

CTAs
Clearer CTAs for conversion

Add prominent, well-placed calls-to-action that move prospects forward without pressure.

Navigation
Enhanced navigation & flow

Restructure the menu and page flow so visitors never feel lost or stuck.

Responsive
Cross-device responsiveness

Ensure the experience holds up on mobile — increasingly where first impressions happen.

The full rebuild resulted in a more credible, scalable, and trustworthy website for a financial-services brand — and one that's easier to maintain long-term.

A system that signals trust.

With a clean foundation, I developed a consistent visual system focused on trust and clarity:

Visual decisions
  • Blue–green–gray financial palette
  • Professional typography scale
  • Structured content rhythm
  • Sections with visual breathing room
  • Modern button and CTA styling
Why these choices
  • Blue/green = financial-industry visual shorthand for stability
  • Typography scale = legibility at every level
  • Rhythm = makes long pages feel intentional
  • Breathing room = signals confidence
  • CTAs = invite action without demanding it

Key improvements that shipped.

The redesign of Mercer Island Capital significantly improved usability, clarity, and credibility. Key improvements include:

More trustworthy first impression

Every choice — typography, spacing, palette, hierarchy — contributes to a more reliable, professional first read.

09 / Expected impact

A foundation for what's next.

The redesigned Mercer Island Capital website is expected to improve user trust, engagement, and conversion through clearer CTAs and a more intuitive layout. The refreshed visual system and simplified navigation better reflect the professionalism of a financial-services brand, helping visitors understand the value faster and navigate with confidence.

Overall, the redesign provides a stronger foundation for future content, growth, and long-term brand credibility — and a website that's now easier for the team to maintain as the firm scales.

Strong in person, weaker online.

LeMay's mission is to preserve and celebrate the automobile's influence on American culture — presenting cars as historical artifacts and design objects. On its own site, the museum calls cars "rolling sculptures," which became a clue for how the brand wants to be read.

HCDE 310 — Interactive Systems Design & Technology Autumn 2025 Solo project Concept, research, development

Tell it how you feel.

Type something like "I'm tired but hopeful" and MoodTune runs it through an emotion-detection model, blends the emotions it finds into a music mood, and returns a matching set of tracks — with optional genre filters and a button to reshuffle.

Role

Solo — concept, research, development, and write-up.

Stack

Python, Flask, HTML/CSS, Hugging Face Inference API, Last.fm API.

Model

roberta-base-go_emotions — 28 emotion labels, not one flat sentiment.

Code

View on GitHub ↗

01 / Problem

Music is sorted by genre, not feeling.

Streaming platforms organize music by genre and artist, not by how the listener feels. Searching a mood on Spotify returns an auto-generated playlist that's hit-or-miss. For someone stressed, tired, or wound up, getting from a feeling to the right song usually means digging through old playlists or guessing.

MoodTune's premise: describe the feeling in your own words, and let a sentiment model and a mood-tagged music database do the matching.

02 / Process

Narrowing the scope.

Sketching. Three concepts — a social fitness tracker, mood-based music, and a combined to-do/calendar/timer. Mood Music was the one I kept coming back to.

First proposal. The Spotify Web API for playlists, a text-sentiment model, and a stretch goal of reading mood from a webcam with Azure's Face API — plus a dashboard with an emoji, a caption, and a mood-history graph.

Revised proposal. I cut it down to one well-understood pipeline: Hugging Face's GoEmotions for emotion detection and Last.fm's tag API for music, served through Flask. Last.fm doesn't require OAuth the way Spotify's playlist endpoints do, which kept the project to one clear path — text in, tagged tracks out.

03 / How it works

Four steps behind one box.

01

Input cleaning

Genre words ("indie," "metal") and request phrasing ("I want," "give me") are stripped with regex, so "I want something upbeat and poppy" doesn't confuse the model with the word "pop."

02

Emotion detection

The cleaned text goes to roberta-base-go_emotions, which scores it against 28 labels like joy, nervousness, grief, and admiration.

03

Weighted tag blending

Instead of taking only the top emotion, every emotion above 0.15 confidence is summed into music-mood tags and normalized into percentages — so "stressed but hopeful" becomes a blend, not a coin flip.

04

Track retrieval

The primary tag plus any genres goes to Last.fm, results are de-duplicated and sampled to ten tracks — which is what "Regenerate Playlist" re-runs without calling the model again.

04 / Trade-offs

Small decisions.

Placeholder art over broken art. Last.fm's images were missing, mismatched, or oddly sized, so every song gets the same flat icon. Less flashy, but nothing looks wrong.

Search links instead of a Spotify integration. Each track links to a Spotify search rather than building a playlist — avoiding a developer app and OAuth login just to resolve a song title.

An honest edge case. "Stomach pain and fatigue" doesn't name a feeling, yet the model called it sadness with high confidence — sentiment models read tone and word association, not just emotion words. And with Pop checked, the list mixed "Hurt" by Johnny Cash with "Party in the U.S.A." — the genre filter widened the pool instead of narrowing it.

06 / What I learned

APIs fail quietly.

Tag coverage is uneven — some Last.fm tags return thousands of tracks, others almost none, so the vocabulary needed fallbacks. Rate limits are real once repeated testing starts. Sentiment has to be simplified to be searchable, which is where the weighted blend earned its keep. External APIs fail quietly, pushing the code toward defensive defaults. And a filter isn't automatically a filter — the next fix would intersect genre and mood instead of merging them.

Per the course's AI disclosure policy, the repository includes an ai.txt file: AI helped with the stylesheet, Flask/Jinja syntax checks, debugging, and the Hugging Face integration.

HCDE 351 — Final project Winter 2026 Annie L., Taylor B., Maria N. UN SDG #12 — Responsible Consumption

Know your limits.

Many people waste water, cleaning resources, and money by guessing how much detergent to pour. Laundry Limits is a simple, low-tech guidance system — a visual guide that maps load size and soil level to the right amount, and a measuring-cup insert that shows where to stop.

Team

Annie Lam, Taylor B., and Maria N.

Methods

Paper prototypes, digital models, high-fidelity fabrication, behavioral prototype.

Tools

SolidWorks, laser cutter, 3D printer.

Tested with

Two participants, before-and-after detergent pours.

01 / Goal

Does guidance change the pour?

We wanted to know whether simple physical and visual tools could help people measure detergent more accurately — without new appliances or complex technology. Each of four prototyping methods tested a different part of the design: whether the guide communicated clearly, whether people could use it correctly, and whether it actually reduced overuse.

02 / Process

Four levels of fidelity.

01

Sketches & paper

Explored the guide's layout and how people would read the relationship between load size, soil level, and detergent amount — before spending time on fabrication.

02

Digital models

A SolidWorks model set the cup's dimensions, shape, and measurement levels; a 2D laser-print file refined the guide's type and visual structure.

03

High-fidelity prototypes

The laser-cut guide and 3D-printed measuring tool let people interact with something close to a final product.

04

Behavioral prototype

During testing, participants decided how much detergent to use with and without the system — so we could watch hesitation, confidence, and decision time across the whole workflow, not just the objects.

03 / Testing

With and without the guide.

Two participants poured detergent for several load-size and soil-level scenarios — first with a plain, unmarked laundry-room cup, then with our guide and cup. We compared each pour to the correct amount, then asked four follow-up questions.

01

The problem was real

With the unmarked cup, the first participant noticeably over-poured. The second came closer on intuition alone.

02

The system worked

With the guide and cup, both found the right level for every scenario. Ease-of-use ratings were 8–8.5 and 7 out of 10, and both said they'd use it in their own laundry room.

03

Room to improve

Color coding could tie the guide's recommendations more clearly to the cup's markings, and a larger, more varied group would surface issues two people can't.

04 / Reflection

Designing for everyday habits.

The idea seemed simple, but prototyping showed how much uncertainty, habit, and quick judgment live inside an everyday task. People understood and liked Laundry Limits, yet still hesitated when their laundry didn't fit neatly into one category. Design isn't just giving people information — it's helping them feel confident deciding in real time.

Moving through fidelity levels mattered too. Sketches let us explore quickly, but watching people handle the 3D-printed cup and laser-cut guide communicated far more than explaining the idea ever could. Next, I'd refine the guide for in-between cases and test with more people.

HCDE 351 — UX Prototyping Techniques Winter 2026 Solo project 120 seconds

A softer goodnight.

Reframing sleep not as an app problem, but as an environmental and behavioral one: being physically tired but mentally alert late at night. Instead of another tracking app, Night Mode is a coordinated sensory shutdown ritual.

Role

Solo — concept, storyboard, filming, acting, editing, testing.

Format

Landscape, live-action, first-person video, 120 seconds.

Tools

Alexa smart lighting, tripod, iMovie.

Evaluated on

Feasibility, usability, and desirability.

01 / Concept

One command, five cues.

"Alexa, start Night Mode" triggers a sequence: lights dim, the phone goes face down, a weighted blanket adds pressure, a sleep mask removes light, and three slow breaths close it out.

The hypothesis: coordinating these cues as a single ritual, rather than isolated habits, signals relaxation more effectively and shortens the time it takes to fall asleep. Rather than adding technology, I reframed two existing objects — a weighted blanket and a sleep mask — as parts of an intentional system.

02 / Process

Designing around real limits.

I built a scene breakdown before filming — problem state, trigger moment, step-by-step shutdown, before and after, calm wake-up — so every interaction stayed visible within 120 seconds. I set the evaluation criteria first so the footage would actually answer my questions, not just demo the idea.

Filming alone shaped the production: a tripod for stable shots, and voiceover planned from the start to explain internal state without awkward on-camera narration. When no Alexa command matched "Night Mode" exactly, I spoke specific commands ("dim lights to 30%") and adjusted color by hand — a reminder that video prototyping often means designing around what the technology can't quite do yet.

03 / What viewers said

Comforting, but fragile.

Participants watched the video and answered open-ended questions about what felt realistic, where they saw friction, and whether they'd try it.

01

Usability

Everyone understood the sequence without explanation. Some asked whether it could be one automated trigger instead of several manual steps.

02

Feasibility

Realistic if you already own the tools. The ritual doesn't travel — it breaks down on vacation or in an unfamiliar room.

03

Desirability

Described as "easy to follow," "comforting," "realistic," and "relatable" — several said they'd try it themselves.

The lighting cue was the strongest moment; viewers read it as a signal to shut down without prompting. The biggest limitation was sustainability — environmental cues support a routine but don't guarantee it night after night.

04 / Critique

Intention over polish.

This was my first structured video prototype, and most hard problems were logistical. Editing in iMovie was new — layering voiceover was tricky, but holding shots slightly longer than felt natural improved the emotional pacing.

The bigger takeaway: a simple cue like dimming lights created a real emotional shift when it was framed clearly. Next time I'd add stronger sound design, test whether an automated lighting trigger feels more believable than my manual workaround, and bring a second person to cue effects.

HCDE 351 — UX Prototyping Techniques Winter 2026 4 prototypes Documented on Medium

Hands-on iteration.

Four weekly prototypes, each in a new fabrication method — and each one taken through multiple iterations shaped by critique and real use.

01 / 3D print

Red Bull lid.

My favorite drink kept spilling when I traveled, so I modeled a snap-on lid in SolidWorks. I printed the first design at three sizes to find the tolerance — a 55 mm diameter with a concentric print pattern fit best — but it wasn't secure and liquid pooled on the rim.

For version two I removed the inner rim edge, added a tab to pull it off, and made it thinner for a tighter press fit. Testing showed PLA wasn't waterproof enough, so the final lid is printed in PETG, which is more water-resistant and flexible enough not to crack.

Classmates said their Red Bulls spill too. I printed extras for friends — one stopped spills in her car's cup holder, another hiked with it in a backpack pocket. I use mine every day. Next, I'd emboss the Red Bull logo on top.

02 / Laser cut

Folding phone stand.

My first time using a laser cutter: a phone stand for filming at different angles, cut from one sheet of cardboard, stored flat, with no fasteners. I sketched six ideas, mocked up my favorite in cardboard, and drew the cut file in Adobe Illustrator.

The breakthrough came at the gym — a bench's adjustment ladder folds flat and locks at angles, which was exactly what the back support needed. Living hinges with a brick pattern made the cardboard bend.

Iteration one's 1 mm hinges were fragile; scaling up in iteration two made them sturdier but the wider lever stopped catching the notches; iteration three restored the lever but wobbled lower on the ladder. Critique pointed to thicker (not wider) notches and a confusing setup — peers tried to push the lever through the notch. They did like that it was all one piece: nothing to lose.

03 / Sewing

Bottle tote.

A sewn container with a closing mechanism — muslin tote bags with snap buttons, and my first time sewing. I sketched five concepts and prototyped three.

Iteration one taught me to fold one long piece of fabric instead of piecing shapes — and that I'd sewn the straps inside out. Iteration two was sized around my water bottle with folded edges and shorter, stronger straps, though a reviewer noted a tall bottle would bump your hand. Iteration three moved to a square shape with longer handles and much cleaner stitching. Next: canvas fabric and a hidden button stitch.

04 / Model

Electric screwdriver.

I started by using my own electric screwdriver and power drill to understand how they feel and operate, then sketched three layouts and picked the most familiar one. I built it from paper, tape, and cardboard tubes — testing plastic, Play-Doh, and rocks before landing on a one-pound lead weight wrapped in foam to get the heft right.

Testing exposed an orientation problem: the smooth cylinder made the trigger hard to find, and the round power button read like a screen control. Next iteration: labels, raised 3D buttons, a taller trigger than the speed buttons, and different base shapes.
